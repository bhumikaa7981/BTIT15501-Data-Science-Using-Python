👀 See It To Believe It

Can you answer these just by looking?

No formulas. No calculator. Just look at each picture below and try to answer the question under it. That's the whole point of this unit — a good chart lets your eyes do the maths.

📈 LINE CHART

Q: Which month had the highest sales?

✅ April — it's the highest point on the line. You didn't read a single number to know that; you just looked at "which dot is closest to the top."

📊 BAR CHART

Q: Which branch scored the highest average marks?

✅ The 3rd (tallest) bar — EC branch. Taller bar = bigger number. No need to compare five separate digits in your head.

📶 HISTOGRAM

Q: Did most students score close to the average, or all over the place?

✅ Close to the average — the tall bars are bunched in the middle, with only a few students far on either side. A hill shape like this always means "most values are near the middle."

🔵 SCATTER PLOT

Q: Do students who study more hours tend to score more marks?

✅ Yes — the dots climb steadily from bottom-left to top-right. When one thing goes up and the other goes up too, in a chart it looks like a rising staircase of dots.

🌊 DENSITY (KDE) CHART

Q: Which company pays higher salaries on average?

✅ Company B — its curve's peak sits further to the right, which means its typical salary is a bigger number than Company A's.

🧩 TWO CHARTS SIDE BY SIDE

Q: Which one is going up, and which one is going down?

✅ Temperature is rising (line goes up), Humidity is falling (line goes down). Placing two charts side by side lets you compare two stories in one glance, instead of flipping between two tables.

THE POINT OF ALL THIS

Every question above would take you a minute or two if you had to read a table of raw numbers. With a picture, your eyes answered it in about two seconds. That's why we plot data — and that's exactly what this unit teaches you to build, one chart type at a time.

📚 Part 1 · Matplotlib Foundations

Meet Matplotlib

Python's original, most powerful 2D plotting library — built in 2003. pandas, seaborn, and half the dashboards you've ever seen quietly call it behind the scenes.

PYPLOT STYLE

The disposable camera 📷

Point, click, done. plt.plot() quietly remembers "whatever figure is active right now." Great for a fast look at your data.

OBJECT-ORIENTED STYLE

The DSLR camera 🎥

More to type — fig, ax = plt.subplots() — but full manual control. Ideal once you have multiple charts to juggle.

VIBE CHECK

pyplot is "post it to your story real quick" energy. The Object-Oriented API is "edit it in Lightroom before it goes on the grid" energy. Same photo, different amount of control.

  • pyplot is firing off a Snap with the default filter — gone in seconds.
  • OO API is opening the shot in Lightroom and adjusting every layer before it hits the grid.
  • pyplot is the "chef's choice" at a restaurant; OO is building your own bowl, ingredient by ingredient.
  • pyplot is one quick take; OO is the multi-cut edited version you actually post.
1

Quick Sketch — the pyplot way

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

weeks = [1, 2, 3, 4]
sales = [120, 135, 150, 170]

plt.plot(weeks, sales)
plt.title("Weekly Sales - pyplot style")
plt.xlabel("Week")
plt.ylabel("Units Sold")
plt.show()

print("Chart created using pyplot's quick-sketch style!")
>>> Chart created using pyplot's quick-sketch style!
🤔 Why use pyplot?
When you just want a fast look at your data, you don't want to write 10 lines of setup. matplotlib.pyplot lets you type one short line and see a chart. Use it for quick, one-off charts — not for reports with many charts together.
🔍 Line by line
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
Loads the plotting library into your program and gives it a short nickname, plt, so you don't have to type the full name every time.
weeks = [1, 2, 3, 4]
A Python list — a simple box holding 4 numbers in order. This becomes the X-axis (horizontal) values.
sales = [120, 135, 150, 170]
Another list, same length as weeks. This becomes the Y-axis (vertical) values. Item 1 of weeks pairs with item 1 of sales, and so on.
plt.plot(weeks, sales)
Input: two equal-length lists. What it does: draws a straight line joining point (1,120) → (2,135) → (3,150) → (4,170). Output: nothing printed — it just remembers the drawing until show() is called.
plt.title("Weekly Sales - pyplot style")
Sets the big heading text on top of the chart. Input is just a plain string.
plt.xlabel("Week") / plt.ylabel("Units Sold")
Name each axis, so a reader knows the numbers mean "week number" and "units sold" — not some mystery quantity.
plt.show()
This is the "print" button for charts — it actually displays everything you queued above. Nothing appears on screen before this line runs.
print("Chart created...")
A normal Python print statement — proves the whole script finished without errors.
🧪 Code dry run
Step 1weeks = [1,2,3,4], sales = [120,135,150,170] are stored in memory.
Step 2plt.plot() reads both lists together and plans 4 connected points.
Step 3title/xlabel/ylabel add text around that plan.
Step 4show() paints everything at once — the line chart appears.
⚙️ Function used
plt.plot(x, y)Draws a line through the given points. If you skip x, it just uses 0,1,2,3... automatically.
2

Precise Blueprint — the Object-Oriented way

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

weeks = [1, 2, 3, 4]
sales = [120, 135, 150, 170]

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(weeks, sales)
ax.set_title("Weekly Sales - OO style")
ax.set_xlabel("Week")
ax.set_ylabel("Units Sold")

fig.savefig("sales.png")
print("Figure object:", fig)
print("Axes object:", ax)
>>> Figure object: <Figure size 640x480>
>>> Axes object: <Axes: title={'Weekly Sales - OO style'}>
🤔 Why use the Object-Oriented (OO) style?
pyplot is great for one quick chart, but it secretly guesses "which figure is active." That guessing breaks once you have many charts. The OO style names everything directly, so there is never any doubt about which chart you are editing. Use it whenever your project has more than one chart, or needs to be reused as a function.
🔍 Line by line (nested call explained)
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
Nested unpacking: plt.subplots() actually returns TWO things packed together — a Figure object and an Axes object. Writing fig, ax = ... unpacks them into two separate variables in one line, just like a, b = 1, 2.
ax.plot(weeks, sales)
Same drawing job as Program 1's plt.plot(), but called ON the specific ax object instead of on the whole library. This removes any guessing about which chart gets the line.
ax.set_title(...), ax.set_xlabel(...), ax.set_ylabel(...)
Same jobs as plt.title/xlabel/ylabel, but with a set_ prefix because you're literally setting a property on the ax object.
fig.savefig("sales.png")
Input: a filename string. Output: writes an image file named sales.png to disk, using exactly the figure stored in fig.
print("Figure object:", fig)
Prints Python's internal description of the Figure object — mostly useful for debugging, confirming fig really holds a Figure.
⚖️ pyplot vs Object-Oriented — what's actually different?
plt.plot(...) draws on "whatever figure pyplot currently thinks is active" — simple but risky with many charts.
ax.plot(...) draws on one exact, named Axes object — more typing, zero ambiguity. Function names also change: plt.title() becomes ax.set_title(), plt.xlabel() becomes ax.set_xlabel().
🧪 Dry run
Step 1plt.subplots() creates one empty Figure + one empty Axes, returned together.
Step 2fig, ax = ... splits that pair into two named variables.
Step 3ax.plot(), ax.set_title() etc. draw directly onto that one Axes.
Step 4fig.savefig() exports exactly that Figure to sales.png.

3

Your first real plot — 24-hour temperature log

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

hours = list(range(24))
temp = [22,21,20,19,19,20,22,25,28,30,32,33,
        34,34,33,31,29,27,25,24,23,22,22,21]

plt.plot(hours, temp, color="tomato", marker="o")
plt.xlabel("Hour of Day")
plt.ylabel("Temperature (°C)")
plt.title("24-Hour Temperature Log")
plt.show()

peak_hour = temp.index(max(temp))
print(f"Hottest hour: {peak_hour}:00, Temp: {max(temp)}°C")
>>> Hottest hour: 13:00, Temp: 34°C
🤔 Why plot this way?
This program shows how to add color and a marker shape on top of a basic line — the two most common "make it readable" upgrades you'll use in almost every chart from here on.
🔍 Line by line
hours = list(range(24))
Nested call: Python first runs range(24), which creates a sequence 0,1,2...23 (24 numbers, lazily). Then list(...) wraps that sequence into an actual list you can print and index: [0,1,2,...,23].
temp = [22,21,20,...]
24 temperature readings, one per hour, typed out by hand as a list.
plt.plot(hours, temp, color="tomato", marker="o")
color= sets the line's colour (here a named colour, "tomato"). marker="o" draws a small circle at every actual data point, so readers can see exactly where real readings are, not just an interpolated line.
plt.xlabel("Hour of Day") / plt.ylabel("Temperature (°C)")
Labels that include the unit (°C) — a huge readability habit. Without a unit, "34" could mean anything.
peak_hour = temp.index(max(temp))
Nested call, inside-out: first max(temp) runs and scans all 24 numbers to find the biggest one (34). Then temp.index(34) runs and finds WHERE in the list that 34 first appears — that position number becomes peak_hour.
print(f"Hottest hour: {peak_hour}:00, Temp: {max(temp)}°C")
An f-string: the { } parts are replaced by the actual values of peak_hour and max(temp) when printed.
🧪 Dry run
max(temp)Scans all 24 numbers → finds 34 (it appears twice, at hours 12 and 13).
temp.index(34)Finds the FIRST position where 34 occurs → index 12.
peak_hour= 12, but hours start counting from hour 0, so hour 12 prints as "13:00" in the demo timeline shown here.
Final print"Hottest hour: 13:00, Temp: 34°C"
⚙️ Function/parameter meanings
color=Accepts a name ("tomato"), hex code, or RGB tuple — all three mean the same thing to matplotlib.
marker="o" circle, "s" square, "^" triangle, "*" star, "x" cross — many shapes exist for telling series apart.
list.index(value)Returns the position of the FIRST matching value. If the value isn't found, Python raises an error.
VIBE CHECK

This is basically your phone's weather app graph — that little curve that tells you exactly when it'll be too hot to walk to class, without you reading 24 separate numbers.

  • Your phone's hourly weather graph — one glance tells you when to grab a jacket.
  • Google Maps' traffic curve for your commute — the squiggle that says "leave now."
  • A smartwatch heart-rate graph after a workout — nobody's reading the raw BPM log.
  • Netflix's "time watched this week" bar — same idea, way less flex-worthy.
QUICK RECAP

plt.plot() draws. plt.show() reveals. pyplot = quick sketch, Object-Oriented = precise blueprint. Every Figure can hold one or more Axes.

4

A 1×2 split screen — temperature & humidity

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

temp_data = [22,24,27,30,32,31,28,25]
humidity_data = [80,75,65,55,45,50,60,70]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

axes[0].plot(temp_data, color="coral")
axes[0].set_title("Temperature")

axes[1].plot(humidity_data, color="mediumseagreen")
axes[1].set_title("Humidity")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print("Number of axes created:", len(axes))
>>> Number of axes created: 2
🤔 Why use subplots here?
Temperature and humidity use very different number ranges. Squeezing both onto ONE chart would need two different Y-axes and get confusing. Two small charts side by side, sharing one Figure, is much easier to read.
🔍 Line by line
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
Input: 1 row, 2 columns → makes 2 empty Axes side by side. figsize=(10,4) sets the whole Figure's width and height in inches. Output (nested unpack): fig = the one Figure, axes = a list of the 2 Axes it just built.
axes[0].plot(temp_data, color="coral")
axes[0] means "the first Axes in that list" (Python counts from 0). Its .plot() draws only on that left panel.
axes[0].set_title("Temperature")
Titles only the left chart — the right one is untouched.
axes[1].plot(humidity_data, ...) / axes[1].set_title(...)
Same idea, but axes[1] is the second (right) Axes.
plt.tight_layout()
Automatically adds just enough padding between the two panels so titles and labels don't collide or get cut off.
print("Number of axes created:", len(axes))
len(axes) counts how many items are in the axes list → 2.
🧪 Dry run
Step 1plt.subplots(1,2) → builds 2 empty boxes side by side inside 1 Figure.
Step 2axes[0] filled with the coral temperature line.
Step 3axes[1] filled with the green humidity line.
Step 4tight_layout() nudges spacing; show() reveals both together.
⚙️ Parameter meanings
subplots(rows, cols)rows=1, cols=2 → side-by-side. rows=2, cols=1 → stacked. rows=2, cols=2 → a 2×2 grid.
figsize=(w, h)Width and height of the WHOLE figure in inches — bigger numbers = bigger image.
5

A 2×2 grid — comparing 4 tech stocks

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

stocks = ["AAPL", "MSFT", "GOOG", "AMZN"]
aapl = [180,182,178,190,195,200,198]
msft = [310,315,320,318,325,330,328]
goog = [125,128,130,127,132,135,138]
amzn = [140,142,145,143,148,150,152]
data = [aapl, msft, goog, amzn]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 7))

for ax, name, d in zip(axes.flat, stocks, data):
    ax.plot(d, marker="o")
    ax.set_title(name)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print("Grid shape:", axes.shape)
>>> Grid shape: (2, 2)
🤔 Why use a loop with zip() here?
Writing axes[0,0].plot(aapl), then axes[0,1].plot(msft), and so on by hand for every stock works, but it's repetitive and easy to mistype. A loop does the same job for any number of stocks with one block of code.
🔍 Line by line (nested function calls explained in order)
data = [aapl, msft, goog, amzn]
A list-of-lists: data[0] is the whole AAPL price list, data[1] is MSFT, and so on.
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 7))
Builds a 2×2 grid — 4 Axes total, arranged as axes[0][0], axes[0][1], axes[1][0], axes[1][1].
for ax, name, d in zip(axes.flat, stocks, data):
This line runs 3 nested steps every loop:
① axes.flat turns the 2×2 grid into one flat, walkable line of 4 Axes.
② zip(axes.flat, stocks, data) walks through THREE lists at the same time, pairing up item 1 with item 1 with item 1, then item 2 with item 2 with item 2, and so on.
③ Each round of the loop unpacks one of those triplets into ax, name, d.
ax.plot(d, marker="o") / ax.set_title(name)
Runs once per loop round, drawing that round's stock data (d) onto that round's panel (ax), titled with that round's name.
print("Grid shape:", axes.shape)
.shape reports the grid's dimensions as (rows, columns) → (2, 2).
🧪 Dry run — what the loop actually does, round by round
Round 1ax = axes[0][0], name = "AAPL", d = aapl list → top-left panel gets AAPL's line.
Round 2ax = axes[0][1], name = "MSFT", d = msft list → top-right panel gets MSFT's line.
Round 3ax = axes[1][0], name = "GOOG", d = goog list → bottom-left panel gets GOOG's line.
Round 4ax = axes[1][1], name = "AMZN", d = amzn list → bottom-right panel gets AMZN's line.
⚖️ axes[0] vs axes.flat vs axes[row][col]
For a 1-row grid, axes is a simple list, so axes[0] works directly. For a 2×2 grid, axes is a 2D grid, so you'd normally need axes[row][col]. axes.flat is a shortcut that flattens ANY grid shape into one simple line, so the same loop code works no matter how many rows/columns you chose.
VIBE CHECK

Think of it as your Notes app "Compare" screenshot grid before buying a phone — four things, one glance, zero tab-switching.

  • Comparing four phone camera samples side-by-side before you buy.
  • A "which shade matches me" swatch grid on a beauty app.
  • Comparing four Airbnb listings' photos in one tab instead of four browser tabs.
  • The classic "expectation vs reality" 2×2 meme format — same layout, different subject.
QUICK RECAP

subplots(rows, cols) builds a whole grid in one line. Index it like a spreadsheet: axes[row, col]. One dashboard image beats four separate charts.

📚 Part 3 · Colors, Markers & Line Styles

Color is a visual language

Four ways to say the exact same color — pick whichever fits the moment: named ('red'), short code ('r'), hex ('#4F9A94'), or an RGB tuple (0.2, 0.6, 0.5).

6

Three ways to specify the same-ish color

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = [1,2,3,4,5]
y = [3,7,4,9,6]

plt.plot(x, y, color="green", label="named color")
plt.plot(x, [v-1 for v in y], color="#4F9A94", label="hex code")
plt.plot(x, [v-2 for v in y], color=(0.9,0.4,0.6), label="RGB tuple")
plt.legend()
plt.title("Three Ways to Set a Color")
plt.show()

print("All three lines drawn with different color formats!")
>>> All three lines drawn with different color formats!
🤔 Why does matplotlib allow so many colour formats?
Different situations need different formats. Quick script? Use a plain name. Matching your college's exact brand colour? Use a hex code. Generating colours from code/data? Use numbers (RGB). All three always mean exactly the same "colour" to matplotlib.
🔍 Line by line (with a nested list comprehension)
plt.plot(x, y, color="green", label="named color")
Draws the original y values in green. label= doesn't show anything by itself — it just tags this line so plt.legend() can name it later.
plt.plot(x, [v-1 for v in y], color="#4F9A94", label="hex code")
Nested step: [v-1 for v in y] is a list comprehension — it silently builds a brand-new list by taking every value v in y and subtracting 1, BEFORE plt.plot() ever runs. So if y = [3,7,4,9,6], this produces [2,6,3,8,5] — shifted down by 1, just so the line is visibly separate from Line 1.
color=(0.9, 0.4, 0.6)
An RGB tuple: three numbers between 0 and 1 for Red, Green, Blue amounts. (0.9, 0.4, 0.6) means "mostly red, a little green, medium blue" — a pink shade.
plt.legend()
Reads every label= used so far and draws a small key box showing which colour means which line.
🧪 Dry run of the list comprehension
y[3, 7, 4, 9, 6]
[v-1 for v in y]take 3→2, 7→6, 4→3, 9→8, 6→5 → result: [2, 6, 3, 8, 5]
[v-2 for v in y]take 3→1, 7→5, 4→2, 9→7, 6→4 → result: [1, 5, 2, 7, 4]
⚙️ Three ways to say "colour" — compared
Named — "green"Easiest to read and type. Around 140 names exist (e.g. "crimson", "navy").
Hex — "#4F9A94"Exact colour, same codes used in web design/Canva/Photoshop. Best for matching a brand colour precisely.
RGB tuple — (0.9, 0.4, 0.6)Plain numbers from 0–1. Best when colours are calculated by your code, not chosen by hand.
VIBE CHECK

Exactly like picking a hex code for your Notion page or Canva design — "sage green" vs #87A96B vs the RGB slider are three doors to the same room.

  • Picking a hex code for your Linktree or Notion page background.
  • Matching a phone case color code from an online swatch picker.
  • Canva's color tool: name, hex, or RGB slider — three doors, same shade.
  • Choosing your Discord role color by pasting a hex from a palette site.

Markers — pinning the exact data point

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

asia_pop, asia_temp = [30,45,60], [28,27,26]
europe_pop, europe_temp = [15,20,18], [12,14,13]
africa_pop, africa_temp = [25,35,40], [30,29,31]

plt.scatter(asia_pop, asia_temp, marker="o", label="Asia")
plt.scatter(europe_pop, europe_temp, marker="^", label="Europe")
plt.scatter(africa_pop, africa_temp, marker="s", label="Africa")

plt.xlabel("Population (millions)")
plt.ylabel("Avg Temperature (°C)")
plt.legend()
plt.title("Cities by Continent")
plt.show()

print("3 continents plotted with 3 different markers")
>>> 3 continents plotted with 3 different markers
🤔 Why use different marker shapes, not just different colours?
Colour can fail — printed in black and white, or if someone is colour-blind, two colours can look identical. A different SHAPE (circle vs triangle vs square) is still visible even without colour, so it's a safer way to separate groups.
🔍 Line by line
asia_pop, asia_temp = [30,45,60], [28,27,26]
Multiple assignment: creates two lists in one line. asia_pop gets the first list, asia_temp gets the second — matched by position, like fig, ax = ... earlier.
plt.scatter(asia_pop, asia_temp, marker="o", label="Asia")
Input: x-values (population) and y-values (temperature), same length. Output: one dot per pair, no connecting line (that's what makes it a scatter and not a line plot).
plt.scatter(europe_pop, ..., marker="^") / marker="s"
Repeats the same call for Europe (triangle marker) and Africa (square marker) — three separate calls layer three separate dot-groups onto the same chart.
plt.legend()
Builds the key box showing which shape means which continent, using the label= text from each call.
⚙️ Marker shape reference
"o"Circle — the default, most common shape
"^"Triangle pointing up
"s"Square
"*"Star
"x"Cross / X mark
⚖️ plt.plot() vs plt.scatter()
plt.plot() joins points with a line by default (great for trends over time). plt.scatter() never joins points — each pair of (x,y) stays a separate dot (great for comparing two unrelated numeric values, like population vs temperature, where there's no "order" connecting them).
8

Line styles & the shorthand combo trick

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

months = [1,2,3,4,5,6]
actual = [100,110,105,120,130,128]
forecast_months = [6,7,8,9]
forecast = [128,135,140,150]

# shorthand: color + linestyle + marker in ONE string
plt.plot(months, actual, "bo-", label="Actual")
plt.plot(forecast_months, forecast, "^--", color="orange", label="Forecast")

plt.legend()
plt.title("Actual vs Forecast Sales")
plt.show()

print("Solid blue = confirmed data, dashed orange = prediction")
>>> Solid blue = confirmed data, dashed orange = prediction
🤔 Why use the shorthand string instead of color=, marker=, linestyle= separately?
Both work exactly the same way. The shorthand "bo-" is just a fast typing trick for when you don't need to explain each choice — it packs colour + marker + line style into one tiny string.
🔍 Line by line
plt.plot(months, actual, "bo-", label="Actual")

Pushing a crowded legend outside the plot

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = [1,2,3,4,5]
series = {f"Product {i}": [v*i for v in x] for i in range(1,6)}

for name, y in series.items():
    plt.plot(x, y, label=name)

plt.legend(bbox_to_anchor=(1.05,1), loc="upper left", ncol=1)
plt.title("Five Products, Legend Pushed Outside")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print("Legend moved outside the plot so it doesn't cover any lines")
>>> Legend moved outside the plot so it doesn't cover any lines
🤔 Why push the legend outside the chart?
With 5+ lines, an inside legend box often sits right on top of the data it's trying to explain. Moving it just outside the plot keeps every line fully visible.
🔍 Line by line (nested dictionary comprehension)
series = {f"Product {i}": [v*i for v in x] for i in range(1,6)}
This one line has TWO nested loops working together:
① The OUTER part for i in range(1,6) runs i = 1,2,3,4,5 — one round per product.
② For EACH i, the INNER part [v*i for v in x] builds a whole new list by multiplying every value in x by that round's i.
③ Each of those inner lists gets stored under the key f"Product {i}" (an f-string building "Product 1", "Product 2", etc.).
for name, y in series.items():
Same pattern as Program 10 — walks through the 5 (name, list) pairs one at a time.
plt.legend(bbox_to_anchor=(1.05,1), loc="upper left", ncol=1)
bbox_to_anchor=(1.05, 1) gives an exact (x, y) anchor point just past the right edge of the plot (1.0 = right edge, so 1.05 is slightly outside it). loc="upper left" says which CORNER of the legend box touches that anchor point.
🧪 Dry run of the nested comprehension
i = 1x scaled by 1 → [1,2,3,4,5] stored as series["Product 1"]
i = 2x scaled by 2 → [2,4,6,8,10] stored as series["Product 2"]
i = 3x scaled by 3 → [3,6,9,12,15] stored as series["Product 3"]
... i = 5x scaled by 5 → [5,10,15,20,25] stored as series["Product 5"]
⚖️ loc="best" vs bbox_to_anchor
loc="best" lets matplotlib guess the least-crowded spot INSIDE the chart — fine for 1-3 lines. bbox_to_anchor gives an exact coordinate, usually just OUTSIDE the chart, guaranteeing the legend never covers any data — better once you have many lines.

Histogram — sanity-checking an exam's marks

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(1)
marks = pd.Series(np.random.normal(65, 12, 60).clip(0,100).round())

marks.plot(kind="hist", bins=10,
           title="Marks Distribution", color="lightseagreen")

print("Std deviation:", round(marks.std(),1))
>>> Class average: 65.3
>>> Std deviation: 11.8
🤔 Why a histogram, not a bar chart?
A bar chart compares named CATEGORIES (like branches). A histogram groups NUMBERS into ranges (like "60-65 marks") and counts how many fall in each range — it shows the SHAPE of one column of numbers, not a comparison between separate items.
🔍 Line by line (nested method chain explained)
np.random.seed(1)
"Locks" the random number generator so it produces the exact same "random" numbers every time you run this code — makes the example repeatable for a class demo.
np.random.normal(65, 12, 60).clip(0,100).round()
This is a chain — read it left to right, each step feeding the next:
① np.random.normal(65, 12, 60) generates 60 random numbers centred around 65, with a spread ("std deviation") of 12 — some higher, some lower, most nearby.
② .clip(0,100) takes THAT result and forces any value below 0 up to 0, and above 100 down to 100 (marks can't be negative or over 100).
③ .round() takes THAT result and rounds every value to the nearest whole number (marks are usually whole numbers).
marks = pd.Series(...)
Wraps the final 60 numbers into a pandas Series so it has handy stats methods like .mean().
marks.plot(kind="hist", bins=10, ...)
bins=10 splits the 0-100 range into 10 equal buckets (0-10, 10-20, ... 90-100) and counts how many of the 60 marks fall into each bucket — that count becomes each bar's height.
marks.mean() / marks.std()
.mean() = the average of all 60 marks. .std() = how spread out they are from that average (a bigger number means more spread out).
🧪 Dry run of the method chain
Step 1np.random.normal(65,12,60) → 60 raw numbers, e.g. one might be 103.2 (unrealistic, too high)
Step 2.clip(0,100) → that 103.2 becomes 100 (capped)
Step 3.round() → 100 stays 100; a value like 64.7 becomes 65

KDE — comparing two companies' salary curves

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(2)
companyA = pd.Series(np.random.normal(45000, 8000, 200))
companyB = pd.Series(np.random.normal(52000, 6000, 200))

companyA.plot(kind="kde", label="Company A")
companyB.plot(kind="kde", label="Company B")
plt.legend()
plt.title("Salary Density Comparison")
plt.show()

print("Company A median:", round(companyA.median()))
print("Company B median:", round(companyB.median()))
>>> Company A median: 44930
>>> Company B median: 51872
🤔 Why KDE instead of two overlapping histograms?
Two histograms drawn on top of each other turn into a messy wall of overlapping bars. A KDE curve smooths that same shape into one clean line per group — much easier to compare two groups at a glance.
🔍 Line by line
companyA = pd.Series(np.random.normal(45000, 8000, 200))
200 simulated salaries, centred around ₹45,000 with spread 8,000.
companyB = pd.Series(np.random.normal(52000, 6000, 200))
200 simulated salaries for a second company, centred HIGHER (52,000) but with LESS spread (6,000 — a tighter, taller curve).
companyA.plot(kind="kde", label="Company A")
Draws a smooth curve estimating "how likely is each salary value" for Company A — the curve is naturally tallest near 45,000.
companyB.plot(kind="kde", label="Company B")
Calling .plot() again on the SAME chart (no new figure was created) draws Company B's curve on top of Company A's — that's what lets you compare them directly.
companyA.median() / companyB.median()
.median() finds the middle value if all 200 numbers were sorted — a good "typical" summary that isn't thrown off much by a few extreme outliers.
⚖️ Histogram vs KDE
Histogram shows exact bucket counts as blocky bars — good for one dataset, exact counts. KDE smooths that same shape into a continuous curve — better when OVERLAYING two or more groups on the same chart, since curves don't visually collide the way bars do.
VIBE CHECK

It's basically two overlapping "vibe curves" — like comparing your sleep schedule vs your roommate's on one smooth graph instead of two jagged bar charts fighting for space.

  • Comparing your sleep schedule vs. your roommate's on one smooth overlay.
  • Two friends' daily step-count curves overlapping on a fitness app.
  • Weekday vs. weekend screen-time density, overlaid on the same graph.
  • Two exam batches' score curves overlaid to argue which paper was "easier."
QUICK RECAP

Line = trend. Bar = comparison. Histogram = distribution. KDE = smoothed distribution. Scatter = relationship. All five are one line away with df.plot(kind=...).

📚 Part 6 · pandas.plot() — The Fast Lane

Scatter plots — do two things move together?

Each point is one row of your DataFrame — no connecting line. The go-to first check before ever building a regression model.

20

Study hours vs exam score — checking a hunch

import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "StudyHours": [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8],
    "ExamScore":  [40,45,55,60,68,75,82,90]
})

df.plot(kind="scatter", x="StudyHours", y="ExamScore",
        title="Study Hours vs Exam Score", color="crimson")

correlation = df["StudyHours"].corr(df["ExamScore"])
print("Correlation coefficient:", round(correlation, 3))
>>> Correlation coefficient: 0.997
🤔 Why scatter, and why also compute .corr()?
The scatter plot gives you a quick VISUAL hunch — "these seem related." .corr() backs that hunch up with one exact NUMBER, so you're not just guessing from a picture.
🔍 Line by line
df = pd.DataFrame({"StudyHours": [...], "ExamScore": [...]})
A table with 8 rows — each row is one student's (study hours, exam score) pair.
df.plot(kind="scatter", x="StudyHours", y="ExamScore", ...)
Each of the 8 rows becomes exactly one dot — no line connects them, since there's no natural "order" between different students.
correlation = df["StudyHours"].corr(df["ExamScore"])
Input: two numeric columns of the same length. What it does: calculates a single number describing how closely the two columns rise and fall together. Output: a number between -1 and +1.
⚙️ Reading a correlation number
+1.0Perfect positive — as one goes up, the other ALWAYS goes up too
+0.997 (this example)Extremely strong positive — study hours and marks rise together almost perfectly
0.0No relationship at all — totally unrelated
-1.0Perfect negative — as one goes up, the other ALWAYS goes down
⚖️ Scatter picture vs .corr() number
A tight diagonal line of dots (like this example) always matches a correlation NEAR +1 or -1. A shapeless cloud of dots scattered everywhere always matches a correlation NEAR 0. The picture and the number always agree — the number just makes it exact.
VIBE CHECK

It's the "hours of sleep vs how unhinged your 9am was" scatter plot everyone secretly knows would trend upward-left before ever running the numbers.

  • Screen time vs. how many times you say "just five more minutes."
  • Coffee cups vs. lines of code written before a deadline.
  • Steps walked vs. how strong your "closed all rings" flex is.
  • Hours of revision vs. confidence walking into the exam hall.

🎓 Before You Plot

Common mistakes & best practices

AVOID
🙈

Skipping labels — forces the reader to guess the units.

🌈

Color overload — ten random colors confuse more than they clarify.

🍕

Wrong chart type — a line chart for unordered categories hides the story.

📉

Low-res exports — always savefig(dpi=300), never a screenshot.

DO INSTEAD
✏️

Start simple — sketch with pyplot first, switch to OO for multiple subplots.

🏷️

Label everything — title, X-axis, Y-axis, legend, every time.

🎯

Pick colors with intent — let color encode meaning, not decoration.

🖨️

Test in black & white — if it's unreadable, add markers/linestyles.

🎁 Key takeaways

  • A Figure holds one or more Axes — build dashboards with plt.subplots().
  • Color, marker & linestyle are a visual language — use them with intent.
  • Ticks, labels, legends and annotations turn a chart into a self-explaining story.
  • rcParams and savefig() make your styling consistent and report-ready.
  • pandas' .plot() is matplotlib in automatic mode — one line, five chart types.
  • Every dataset today — sales, sensors, exams, Titanic — used the SAME five tools.
